# History of Computers

<Lede>
Nobody sat down and invented the computer. It arrived in pieces, over four
thousand years, as people kept running into the same problem: too many numbers,
too little time. This topic follows that line — who built what, in which year,
and which of their ideas your laptop still uses today.
</Lede>

## Overview

Every machine on this page exists because counting by hand failed at some
scale. A merchant outgrew his fingers and reached for an **abacus**. A tax
office outgrew clerks and reached for **punched cards**. An army outgrew desk
calculators and built **ENIAC**. The pattern repeats; only the technology
changes.

<Callout kind="def" title="Definition">
  The history of computers is the record of how calculating devices developed
  from manual aids, through mechanical and electromechanical machines, to the
  electronic stored-program computers in use today.
</Callout>

## Key Concepts

### The manual and mechanical age

<Diagram
  type="chain"
  label="Fig 2"
  title="From counting beads to a calculating machine"
  caption="Each machine did one thing more than the one before it."
  items={[
    { title: 'ABACUS', lines: ['c. 3000 BC', 'counts, does not calculate'] },
    { title: "NAPIER'S BONES", lines: ['1617, John Napier', 'multiplication by rods'] },
    { title: 'PASCALINE', lines: ['1642, Blaise Pascal', 'first adding machine'] },
    { title: 'STEPPED RECKONER', lines: ['1673, G. W. Leibniz', 'also multiplies, divides'], highlight: true },
  ]}
/>

| Device | Year | Inventor | What it could do first |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Abacus** | c. 3000 BC | Mesopotamia, then China | Recorded numbers on beads; the operator did the thinking |
| **Napier's bones** | 1617 | John Napier | Turned multiplication into simple addition of rods |
| **Slide rule** | 1622 | William Oughtred | Multiplied and divided using logarithm scales |
| **Pascaline** | 1642 | Blaise Pascal | Added and subtracted by gears — the first true calculator |
| **Stepped Reckoner** | 1673 | Gottfried Leibniz | Multiplied and divided as well; Leibniz also championed binary |
| **Jacquard loom** | 1801 | Joseph Marie Jacquard | Took its instructions from punched cards — a stored program in cloth |

<Callout kind="note" title="Why a loom belongs in a computer syllabus">
  The Jacquard loom was not a calculator at all. It mattered because it proved
  a machine could follow a set of instructions fed in from outside, and be
  given a different job by changing the cards. Babbage borrowed the idea
  directly.
</Callout>

### Charles Babbage and the machine that was never built

Babbage designed the **Difference Engine** in 1822 to print error-free
mathematical tables, then abandoned it for something far more ambitious. The
**Analytical Engine** of 1837 was the first design for a general-purpose
computer — a machine that would do whatever the cards told it to do.

The engine was never finished — Victorian workshops could not machine the parts
to the tolerance it needed — but the design was right, and it is the reason
Babbage is called the **Father of the Computer**.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Two names, one mark each">
  **Charles Babbage** — Father of the Computer, for the Analytical Engine.
  **Ada Lovelace** — the world's first programmer, for the 1843 notes in which
  she wrote a step-by-step method for the engine to compute Bernoulli numbers,
  and pointed out it could work on symbols, not just numbers.
</Callout>

### The electromechanical age

| Machine | Year | Built by | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tabulating machine** | 1890 | Herman Hollerith | Processed the US census in about two years instead of eight; his company grew into **IBM** |
| **Z3** | 1941 | Konrad Zuse | First programmable machine to work using binary and relays |
| **Harvard Mark I** | 1944 | Howard Aiken, with IBM | 15 metres long, driven by relays and a rotating shaft; ran for fifteen years |

### The electronic age

Relays click; valves do not move at all. Replacing one with the other made
machines roughly a thousand times faster, and started the era we are still in.

<Diagram
  type="chain"
  label="Fig 4"
  title="The four machines that set the pattern"
  eyebrow="1944 TO 1951, IN ORDER"
  caption="Read them as a sequence of firsts: first automatic, first electronic, first stored-program, first sold."
  items={[

    { title: 'EDSAC', lines: ['1949 · Maurice Wilkes', 'first practical stored program'], highlight: true },
    { title: 'UNIVAC I', lines: ['1951 · Eckert, Mauchly', 'first sold commercially'] },
  ]}
  footnote="ENIAC was reprogrammed by rewiring it by hand; EDSAC kept its program in memory, like every machine since."
/>

<Callout kind="def" title="The stored-program concept">
  Proposed by John von Neumann in his 1945 report on the EDVAC: keep the
  program in the same memory as the data, so the machine can be given a new job
  by loading new instructions instead of rewiring it. This is the single idea
  that separates a computer from a calculator.
</Callout>

### Three inventions that shrank the machine

| Invention | Year | Who | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Transistor** | 1947 | Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley (Bell Labs) | Replaced the valve — smaller, cooler, far more reliable |
| **Integrated circuit** | 1958 | Jack Kilby (TI), Robert Noyce (Fairchild) | Put many components on one chip of silicon |
| **Microprocessor** | 1971 | Intel 4004 team, led by Ted Hoff | Put an entire CPU on one chip, which made the personal computer possible |

The rate of that shrinking was noticed early. **Moore's law**, stated by Gordon
Moore in 1965, says the number of transistors on a chip roughly doubles every
two years:

<Eq block>{String.raw`N(t) = N_0 \cdot 2^{\,t/2}`}</Eq>

where <Eq>{String.raw`N_0`}</Eq> is the count today and <Eq>{String.raw`t`}</Eq>
is the number of years ahead. It is an observation about industry, not a law of
physics — and it has been slowing since about 2015.

### Computers in India

| Milestone | Year | Detail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **First computer in India** | 1955 | HEC-2M, installed at the Indian Statistical Institute, Kolkata |
| **First computer built in India** | 1960 | TIFRAC, built at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research, Mumbai |
| **First Indian-made commercial series** | 1970s | The Siddhartha and TDC series from ECIL, Hyderabad |
| **First Indian supercomputer** | 1991 | PARAM 8000, built by C-DAC under Vijay Bhatkar |

<Callout kind="warn" title="Careless-mistake trap">
  ENIAC is the first general-purpose **electronic** computer, not the first
  computer. UNIVAC I is the first **commercially sold** computer, not the first
  built. Mark I is **electromechanical**, not electronic. Half the marks in this
  topic are lost on those three words.
</Callout>

## Example

A timeline is just data, and data is something you can sort. This prints the
milestones in order and works out how long each gap was — which is where the
story becomes visible.

```python
# 1. INPUT — the milestones as (year, machine) pairs
timeline = [
    (1642, "Pascaline"),
    (1837, "Analytical Engine"),
    (1946, "ENIAC"),
    (1971, "Intel 4004"),
]

# 2. PROCESS — the gap between each machine and the next
for i in range(len(timeline) - 1):
    year, name = timeline[i]
    next_year, next_name = timeline[i + 1]
    gap = next_year - year
    print(f"{name} to {next_name}: {gap} years")

# 3. OUTPUT — how fast the last stretch moved
print("Span of the whole story:", timeline[-1][0] - timeline[0][0], "years")
```

Expected output:

```text
Pascaline to Analytical Engine: 195 years
Analytical Engine to ENIAC: 109 years
ENIAC to Intel 4004: 25 years
Span of the whole story: 329 years
```

Notice the gaps shrinking: 195 years, then 109, then 25. Progress in computing
does not move at a steady speed — each machine makes the next one easier.
